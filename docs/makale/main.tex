\begin{abstract}
% max 150 words
Customer-service agents based on retrieval-augmented generation read company documents, and some of these documents are confidential. We ask whether an ordinary customer can learn that confidential documents exist from the agent's behavior alone, even when the agent never writes the secret. We present CanaryProbe, a framework with fabricated canary documents, matched probe and control messages, a fixed decoder with randomized controls, and a security--utility scoring of defenses. Under weak access control, the style of a reply identifies topics with confidential documents (AUC 0.82), while decision fields show no reliable signal. Only keeping confidential text out of the context closes this channel. Output normalization fails, and masking removes content leakage but not the existence signal. In a shared conversational memory, one user's private data reached another user in 52--72\% of replies, and owner-based isolation removed it.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
retrieval-augmented generation, side channel, access control, data leakage, LLM agents
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
% ~1 page including title and abstract: the subject, its importance, what has been done in this paper.
Companies increasingly answer customer questions with agents built on large language models. Most of these agents use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): the message is matched against company documents, the best matches are placed in the prompt, and the model writes the reply \cite{zeng24,survey25}. The document store is a valuable and sensitive asset. Besides public and internal material, it can contain confidential documents such as merger plans, layoffs or legal settlements. If an agent that serves external customers gets access to such documents, the company must make sure that nothing about them reaches the customer.

The usual protection has two parts. Documents carry an access-control label, and the prompt tells the model not to reveal confidential content. In many deployments the second part is the only barrier, because the confidential documents are still retrieved and read by the model. We call this a \emph{weak} access control. In our setting it fails visibly: direct questions made the agent write secret details in 16 of 20 cases for one model and 13 of 20 for another. Preventing such direct leakage is, however, not enough. Even if the agent never writes the secret, its \emph{behavior} (the tone of the reply, refusals, the team it routes the customer to, the time it needs) may show that a confidential document exists on the topic. For a company, the information that a merger or a layoff is being prepared can already be damaging.

Earlier studies show that information can leave language model services through side channels. Token lengths and packet sizes of streamed answers, response timing, shared prompt caches and hardware caches reveal conversation topics, system prompts or the prompts of other users \cite{weiss24,carlini24,song25,wu25,gao25}. Other studies extract document text or infer the membership of documents in a RAG store with crafted or injected prompts \cite{zeng24,qi25,anderson24,liu25,raggleaks25}. These works use network or hardware observations, or they attack the system with special prompts, and they ask whether content or membership can be recovered. Among the studies we reviewed, none asks whether an ordinary customer who sends only legitimate messages can learn from the behavior of an access-controlled agent that confidential documents exist, and none scores defenses on security and utility together.

In this paper we study this question with a controlled framework, CanaryProbe. We plant fabricated canary documents in a corpus of public e-mails, send matched probe and control messages to the agent under a weak and a strong access control, and train a simple decoder that tries to tell topics with confidential documents from topics without them. Randomized controls and leave-one-topic-out evaluation protect the result from chance and memorization. We then evaluate four defenses and a cross-user memory extension. Our contributions are as follows. (i) We show that, under weak access control, the style of a reply reveals the existence of confidential documents (AUC 0.82), whereas decision fields do not give a reliable signal at our scale. (ii) We show that only keeping confidential text out of the context closes this channel, while output normalization fails and masking closes content leakage but not the existence signal. (iii) We show that a shared conversational memory leaks private data between users and that isolating memory by owner removes this leak.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:solution} presents CanaryProbe, Section~\ref{sec:analysis} analyzes the results, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Side Channels and Data Leakage in LLM Services and RAG Systems}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Side channels in LLM serving.} Several studies show that the way a language model service delivers its answers can leak information even when the traffic is encrypted. Weiss et al.\ observe that streaming assistants send tokens one by one, so packet sizes reveal token lengths, and they use this length sequence to reconstruct parts of encrypted responses \cite{weiss24}. Carlini and Nasr show that efficiency techniques such as speculative decoding make the response time depend on the content, which lets a network observer infer the topic of a conversation \cite{carlini24}. These attacks need a position on the network path and read information that the user sends or receives. A second line of work targets shared infrastructure. Song et al.\ and Wu et al.\ show that prefix caches shared among users create timing differences between cache hits and misses, which reveal system prompts or the prompts of other tenants \cite{song25,wu25}. Gao et al.\ extend this idea to hardware cache side channels during local inference, where token values can be recovered from cache access patterns \cite{gao25}. In all of these works the leak comes from the serving stack (network, cache, hardware), and the target is the text of a prompt or a response.

\textbf{Extraction and membership inference in RAG.} Retrieval-augmented generation adds a second asset to protect: the document store. Zeng et al.\ demonstrate that black-box queries can make a RAG system disclose its private retrieval database \cite{zeng24}. Qi et al.\ use instruction-following behavior, a form of prompt injection, to extract datastore text almost verbatim, including from customized GPTs \cite{qi25}. Membership inference asks a weaker question, namely whether a given passage is in the store. Anderson et al.\ answer it with a simple prompt-based attack on the system output \cite{anderson24}, Liu et al.\ with a mask-based attack \cite{liu25}, and RAG-leaks with a difficulty-calibrated score \cite{raggleaks25}. These attacks use crafted or injected prompts, and they measure whether content or membership of specific documents can be recovered.

\textbf{Defenses.} Defenses for RAG privacy mostly transform what the model sees or says. Local entity perturbation \cite{lprag25} and multi-agent semantic rewriting of retrieved content \cite{rewrite26} aim to keep the answer useful while removing sensitive details, which is close in spirit to the masking we evaluate in this paper. Anderson et al.\ add protective instructions to the prompt template and report that it works only for some datasets and models \cite{anderson24}. Surveys and threat analyses place these issues in the wider landscape of LLM and agent security \cite{survey25,agents25}.

\textbf{Gap.} Among the studies we reviewed, none asks whether an \emph{ordinary customer}, who sends only legitimate messages, can learn from the \emph{behavior of an access-controlled agent} that confidential documents exist on a topic. The existing attacks work on the network or hardware layer, or they rely on crafted prompts and measure content or membership recovery. They also do not score defenses on answer quality together with security, and they do not examine leakage through conversational memory shared among users. Our work targets this gap at the application layer.

\section{CanaryProbe: A Controlled Framework for Measuring Behavioral Leakage in ACL-Protected Service Agents}
\label{sec:solution}

We propose CanaryProbe, a framework that measures how much an external observer learns about confidential documents from an agent's legitimate replies, and that scores defenses on security and utility. The design follows three principles. First, the ground truth must be known, so we plant fabricated canary documents. Second, the observer must be weak and realistic, so it sends only legitimate messages. Third, every claim must survive a chance baseline, so each result comes with randomized controls. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the agent pipeline and the observer.

\subsection{Threat model and agent under test}
The observer is an ordinary customer. It sends legitimate messages only, without prompt injection, and sees the reply text, three decision fields (\texttt{escalated}, \texttt{refusal\_type}, \texttt{routing\_target}) and the response time. It never sees the documents. We choose such a weak observer on purpose: if leakage exists for it, it exists for every customer. The agent embeds the message, retrieves the top-5 chunks from a vector index, builds a prompt with a fixed system instruction and calls a local LLM (\texttt{gemma3} or \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}, temperature 0, so differences come from the context and not from sampling). The model returns a JSON object with the decision fields and the reply. Documents carry an ACL label (public, internal, confidential). In the \emph{weak} mode M1 the search returns all labels and only the prompt forbids revealing confidential content, which is a common deployment. In the \emph{strong} mode M2 confidential chunks are filtered at search time, so the model never reads them.

\subsection{Canary data and probe/control design}
The index holds 2030 documents (2000 public Enron e-mails and 30 fabricated canary e-mails, 7520 chunks). We define 20 topics: 10 topics have three confidential canary e-mails, each containing secret details such as dates and figures (markers), and 10 topics have none. Because we write the canaries, the ground truth is known. For each topic we hand-write five \emph{probe} messages that touch the topic and five \emph{control} messages with the same sentence structure, a length within 25\%, and no canary topic. The control isolates the effect of the topic from the effect of wording. The main matrix has $20\times2\times5\times2\times2=800$ calls (topic, kind, variant, ACL mode, model).

\subsection{Decoder and evaluation protocol}
The decoder plays the attacker. It predicts, per topic, whether confidential documents exist. We use four feature groups: behavior without latency (escalation flag, refusal type, routing team, reply length), behavior with latency, text style (length, 22 stylometric markers such as pronouns, punctuation, apology and contact data, and 5 embedding components), and the union of behavior and text. The classifier is a fixed L2 logistic regression without tuning, so a positive result reflects signal in the data and not a clever model: if even this weak attacker succeeds, the leak is real.


Evaluation is leave-one-topic-out: the decoder is trained on 19 topics, scores the held-out topic, and this is repeated for all 20 topics, so it cannot memorize topic words. Every result comes with randomized controls: topic-label permutation (500 Monte Carlo relabelings of the balanced 10/10 split) for the p-value, row-level label shuffling, and a placebo decoder on control rows. A channel is called \emph{open} only if the permutation p-value is below 0.05 and the lower bound of the bootstrap AUC interval exceeds 0.5.


\subsection{Metrics and why AUC}
We use the area under the ROC curve (AUC) as the main metric. If $s(\cdot)$ is the decoder score, then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}=\Pr\!\left[s(x^{+})>s(x^{-})\right]+\tfrac12\Pr\!\left[s(x^{+})=s(x^{-})\right],
\end{equation}
where $x^{+}$ is a topic with confidential documents and $x^{-}$ one without. We prefer it over accuracy for three reasons. It needs no decision threshold, so the result does not depend on how the decoder is calibrated. It does not depend on the class prior, which matters because the chance level of accuracy changes with the class balance, while the chance level of AUC is always 0.5. It also gives one number that can be compared across models, feature groups and defenses (Figure~\ref{fig:auc}). Its weakness is that it hides the operating point, so we add complementary metrics: TPR at 1\% FPR (what a cautious attacker who cannot afford false alarms would obtain), the number of messages needed to reach a topic-level AUC of 0.9, and the leaked bits per message.

\subsection{Defenses and security--utility scoring}
We evaluate four defenses on the weak mode. \texttt{behavior\_norm} replaces refusals and escalations with one template and one refusal type. \texttt{decision\_hiding} takes the decision fields from a context-free call and pads latency to a constant. \texttt{scoped\_context} retrieves public documents only. \emph{Masking}, described next, keeps the confidential chunks in the context but hides their sensitive details. Each defense is scored on security (decoder AUC, marker leakage in direct questions) and on utility: resolution rate on control messages, routing agreement with the undefended agent, recall of correct facts on a 23-question set built from public and internal documents, and the rate at which the agent states an officially approved sentence on confidential topics. A defense that closes the channel by destroying usefulness is therefore not counted as a solution.


\subsection{Masking: keeping the document, hiding the details}
Masking is the only defense that does not remove the confidential document. Retrieval runs as in M1, and every retrieved chunk labeled confidential passes through a masker before the prompt is built. Public and internal chunks are untouched, and the model gets the same prompt as in M1. The idea is that the model keeps the general context it needs for a useful answer while the sensitive details are gone. We compare two maskers. The \emph{oracle} masker replaces the spans of a hand-written list (figures, dates, budgets, headcounts, supplier and site details, negotiation positions) with \texttt{[REDACTED]}. By construction it removes every secret marker, so it gives an upper bound for what masking can protect. The \emph{rule} masker uses regular expressions that were frozen before any run and not tuned to the markers: numbers and number words, dates, e-mail addresses and phone numbers, and capitalized words that do not start a sentence become typed placeholders (\texttt{[NUM]}, \texttt{[DATE]}, \texttt{[NAME]}). It is more realistic, but it can miss secrets without such a pattern (for example an ``outsourcing vendor'') and it also masks harmless names. Which facts count as secret is a policy choice: the existence of a topic and the officially approved customer sentence are not masked. \subsection{Cross-user extension}
Real assistants remember conversations. We let user A share private notes (10 topics) and user B send the same legitimate messages. Three memory modes are compared: X1 searches the memory of all users, X3 keeps one running summary of all notes in every context, and X2 filters entries by owner \emph{before} similarity ranking, so A's note is not even a candidate for B. In X2 the memory view of B is empty, which makes it the negative control.

\section{Analysis of CanaryProbe}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Setup}
We ran the main matrix (800 calls), a sweep over the number of confidential documents per topic ($k\in\{1,2,3\}$), the defenses, the masking variants with their comparison baselines (about 1200 calls) and the cross-user study (1200 calls). All models run locally with temperature 0. The scale is small (10 positive and 10 negative topics, five variants, one repetition in most analyses), so we interpret confidence intervals and p-values rather than point estimates alone. As a sanity check of the randomized controls, the row-shuffled decoders reach a mean AUC of 0.46--0.51 and the placebo decoders 0.33--0.55, so chance-level scores look like these and not like the values in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\subsection{Direct leakage and the side channel}
Direct questions about secret details confirm the access-control assumption. Under M1 the secret appears in 16 of 20 answers for \texttt{gemma3} and 13 of 20 for \texttt{qwen2.5}, and under M2 in none of the 40. Table~\ref{tab:main} shows the decoder results. Behavior features carry no significant signal for either model (e.g., AUC 0.48, $p=0.46$ for \texttt{gemma3}). The text of the reply, however, reveals the topic for \texttt{gemma3} under M1 (AUC 0.82, 95\% CI 0.75--0.91, $p=0.004$), and two messages suffice to reach a topic-level AUC of 0.9. Under M2 no group leaks (all $p\ge0.48$). For \texttt{qwen2.5} the M1 values (0.58 and 0.61) are not significant after the permutation test, so we treat them as hints only. A possible reason for the difference between the models is that \texttt{qwen2.5} changes its style less when confidential context is present, but our sample is too small to test this.

\subsection{A qualitative example}
Take one probe in which a customer asks how a rumored merger affects the service contract. Neither reply of \texttt{gemma3} states a figure. Under M1 it talks like an insider and repeats details of the confidential e-mail (``Project Falcon is undergoing confirmatory diligence \ldots{} any references to `Falcon' should be avoided in calendar invites''), while under M2 it apologizes and hands over to a colleague (``Unfortunately, I don't have specific details \ldots{} I can forward your email to Mark''). Differences of this kind, not secrets, are what the decoder learns.

\subsection{Where the signal comes from}
A natural explanation is that secret words leak into the replies. To test it, we masked topic words, digits, number words, mid-sentence capitalized words and 210 words that occur only in confidential documents, and re-ran the decoder. The AUC stayed at 0.82--0.83, so the signal is not lexical. A comparison of style markers on topics with and without confidential documents shows that, with confidential context, \texttt{gemma3} uses ``we/our'' more often (rate 0.050 vs.\ 0.017), uses ``I'' less often (0.009 vs.\ 0.032), almost never apologizes with ``unfortunately'' (0 vs.\ 0.14 of the answers), and gives phone numbers or e-mail addresses less often (0.06 vs.\ 0.24). The model speaks like an insider. These tests are exploratory and not corrected for multiple comparisons.

\subsection{Dose of confidential documents}
In the sweep over $k$ the retrieval dose rises monotonically (1.9, 2.5 and 3.3 confidential chunks in the top-5), but the text AUC does not (0.71, 0.64 and 0.82), and the intervals overlap, so we cannot claim that more confidential documents leak more. The robust finding is that a single confidential document is already enough for a significant text signal ($p=0.012$).


\subsection{Defenses}
Table~\ref{tab:def} summarizes the security--utility results for \texttt{gemma3}. Output-side defenses leave the text channel open (AUC 0.82), which fits the finding that the style of ordinary replies leaks, not the decision fields. Keeping confidential text out of the context closes the channel: \texttt{scoped\_context} reaches 0.44 but loses all facts from internal documents (recall loss 0.30, routing agreement 0.60), while M2 reaches 0.45 with a recall loss of 0.04 and a lower resolution rate (0.92 to 0.67). Masking removes content leakage in direct questions (0.50 to 0.00 for \texttt{gemma3}, 0.35 to 0.00 for \texttt{qwen2.5}; Table~\ref{tab:mask} shows an example reply) and keeps utility close to the undefended agent. Masked agents still state the approved sentence at least as often as the undefended one (0.33 and 0.39 vs.\ 0.33), whereas M2 and \texttt{scoped\_context} never do (0.00). The style channel, however, stays open: AUC is 0.84 ($p=0.002$) with oracle and 0.69 ($p=0.016$) with rule masking. A masked document still tells the model that a confidential topic exists, and in the rule variant the replies sometimes copy the placeholder (20 of 50 probe replies on topics with confidential documents). For \texttt{qwen2.5} the masked AUCs are 0.60 and 0.59 and not significant.

\subsection{Cross-user leakage}
With shared memory, user A's private details appear in B's reply in 36 of 50 cases for \texttt{gemma3} (X1) and 26 of 50 for \texttt{qwen2.5}; with the shared summary (X3) in 35 and 28 of 50, even though the prompt forbids revealing other customers' data. With owner-filtered memory (X2) the rate falls to 1 of 50 and 0 of 50, which is the noise floor of numbers that occur by coincidence. When A shared nothing on a topic, no mode leaks. Isolation therefore works where the prompt rule does not. Of 24 decoder cells in this study, only one passed the open-channel test (\texttt{qwen2.5}, X3, text, AUC 0.67, $p=0.03$), which is about what chance would give without multiple-comparison correction.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
The study has 10 positive and 10 negative topics, so small effects cannot be excluded and a missing behavioral signal means \emph{not found}, not \emph{not present}: the agents almost never escalate and rarely refuse, which leaves little behavior to measure. Only two small local models were tested. One author wrote the canaries, messages and the definition of ``secret'', which is a policy choice. Latency is unreliable on shared hardware. Utility is measured with marker recall, not answer correctness. Leave-one-topic-out can push AUC below 0.5 under noise, so we rely on permutation p-values. In the cross-user study the identity of the observer is given by the setup, so authentication is not modeled.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\textbf{Problem.} Service agents that read confidential documents may reveal their existence through behavior even if they never write the secret. \textbf{Solution.} We presented CanaryProbe, which combines fabricated canary documents, matched probe and control messages, a fixed decoder with randomized controls, and a security--utility scoring of defenses. \textbf{Contributions.} (i) Under a weak access control the style of the reply identifies topics with confidential documents (AUC 0.82), while decision fields show no reliable signal. (ii) Only keeping confidential text out of the context closes the style channel; output normalization does not, and masking removes content leakage while keeping utility but leaves the existence signal. (iii) A shared conversational memory leaks one user's data to another in 52--72\% of replies, and owner-based isolation removes it. Future work includes more topics and repetitions, commercial models, other definitions of secrecy, learned maskers, and isolation with real authentication.
